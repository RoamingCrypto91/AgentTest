business concept · v0.1 · september 2026

The Belonging Ladder

A repeatable, sellable model for building communities, priced on the rung it moves rather than the hours it takes.

the opening

Two facts, and the gap between them

People have started paying for community. Almost nobody can say what one is.

That gap is the business. When a market wants something it cannot define, the most valuable thing on offer is the definition — and the person who supplies it gets to set the terms of every conversation that follows. It is how EOS came to own small-business operations and how StoryBrand came to own marketing messages. Name a small number of parts, put them in an order, add a test at the front, and sell the naming.

The frameworks that already exist do not fill this gap, because they were built for a different buyer. The Community Roundtable's Maturity Model scores an organisation on eight internal competencies. The SPACES Model exists to justify a community's budget to a finance director. The Orbit Model was built for developer relations teams. All three assume the buyer already knows they want a community and needs to measure or defend it.

Your buyer is upstream of all of that. They have an audience, a nagging sense that a community would be worth having, and no model of what it is. They cannot be sold a maturity score. They can be sold a description of what their members are going through.

the market, briefly

What it is worth and what is failing

90%of Discord servers are reported as ghost towns within three months of launch
83%of new servers never pass 100 members, because operators build channels before they build a reason to return
20–30%of members active per month is what a healthy server looks like, so nine in ten quiet members is normal, not a failure

Those three are industry figures from platform and agency sources rather than peer-reviewed research, so quote them as the trade's own numbers and not as findings. They are still the right frame for a sales conversation, because every one of them describes something the buyer has already lived through.

The money is real and the current shape of it is time-based. Creator Discord management runs at roughly €1,000–3,000 a month; broader community retainers at €1,000–5,000 for small clients and €5,000–15,000 for mid-market; strategic consulting bills at €100–200 an hour. Every one of those is a wage in disguise, and every one of them caps out at the number of hours you have. The framework is what lets you leave that pricing model without leaving the market.

the framework

Five things a member needs, in order

A member of any community, on any platform, is asking five things in sequence. Each one has to be answered before the next one gets asked. Each has a mechanism from behavioural science underneath it, a named failure when it goes wrong, and one number that says whether it is working.

1 Notice me 2 Answer me 3 Expect me 4 Know me 5 Need me

The order is the whole product. It means you can say you are stuck on rung two, which is the single most sellable sentence in consulting: it tells the buyer where they are, what is next, and that everything they are currently trying is being poured through a hole further up.

1Notice meThe Foyer
2Answer meThe Void
3Expect meThe Carousel
4Know meThe Throne Room
5Need meThe One-Man Band

Left: what the member is asking. Right: where they end up when nobody answers. You diagnose the lowest rung that is not holding.

RUNG 01
“Notice me.” I have just walked in. Is anything here for me, and what am I supposed to do first?
Mechanism
Behaviour needs motivation, ability and a prompt at the same moment (Fogg). A newcomer has the motivation — they clicked. What is missing is ability and a prompt. A rules wall and eleven channels remove both, and an open invitation to “introduce yourself” is expensive because it asks a stranger to compose an identity before they can speak.
Failure
The Foyer — people arrive, look around a well-organised empty building, and leave. Membership numbers rise the whole time.
Number
share of joiners who ever post and the median hours before a first post.
The fix
One question at the door that a stranger can answer in ten words. Delete the gauntlet. Show evidence of life before you ask for anything.
RUNG 02
“Answer me.” I said something. Did anyone notice?
Mechanism
Reciprocity is close to a human universal (Gouldner), and the newcomer chapter of Kraut and Resnick's evidence review lands on the same place: an unacknowledged first contribution is the most reliable way to lose a member. The person concludes, correctly, that nobody was waiting for them.
Failure
The Void — first posts land in silence. This is the cheapest failure to fix and the most common one to find.
Number
share of first posts answered within an hour, and separately, how many of those answers came from a member rather than from you.
The fix
Named people on a greeting rota with a stated response time. Not a bot. A person, using their name, responding to what was actually said.
RUNG 03
“Expect me.” Is there a reason to come back on Thursday?
Mechanism
A habit is a cue, a routine and a reward, and the cue has to recur predictably or no habit forms. Intentions attached to a specific time and place get acted on far more often than intentions in general (Gollwitzer's implementation intentions). Five irregular things produce no habit in anybody; one fixed thing does.
Failure
The Carousel — constant arrivals replacing constant departures. The dangerous one, because the headline numbers hold up while nobody who joins is still there a month later.
Number
30-day cohort retention: of everyone who joined in January, how many were still posting in March.
The fix
Exactly one recurring event, same day, same hour, announced in advance and summarised afterwards. Plus a personal nudge at day three and day ten, written by a human.
RUNG 04
“Know me.” Is there anybody here I would miss?
Mechanism
Relatedness is one of the three basic psychological needs in self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan), and shared emotional connection between members — not between members and the brand — is one of the four components of a sense of community (McMillan and Chavis, 1986). Weak ties do most of the structural work (Granovetter). Dunbar's layers say why: a group holds together in circles of roughly five, fifteen, fifty and a hundred and fifty, and nobody belongs to a circle of eight thousand.
Failure
The Throne Room — every thread of connection runs through the host. The members are an audience standing in a room together. If the host goes quiet they have no reason to stay, because there is nobody here they know.
Number
share of interactions that are member-to-member, and the share of members who have only ever interacted with staff.
The fix
Introduce two members to each other every week, with a stated reason, in public. And wait thirty minutes before answering anything a member could answer: standing is earned in the gap you leave.
RUNG 05
“Need me.” Does this place want something from me?
Mechanism
Competence and autonomy are the other two needs in self-determination theory. Small commitments make larger ones easier to accept (foot-in-the-door, Freedman and Fraser), and people value what they helped build out of proportion to its quality (the IKEA effect). Ostrom's work on durable commons adds the governance half: the groups that last are the ones whose members set and police their own rules, with graduated consequences rather than arbitrary ones.
Failure
The One-Man Band — nothing happens unless you are in the room. Whatever you have built is a job, not an asset, and it will end when you burn out.
Number
member activity on the days you post nothing, and how few people account for half the conversation.
The fix
Give your existing connectors a title and one specific responsibility each. Hand a recurring format to a member. Then announce a fortnight away and take it.

Why a member's journey, and not an organisation's maturity

Three findings sit underneath the whole ladder. Participation in any online community is wildly unequal, roughly on the order of ninety per cent reading, nine per cent occasionally contributing and one per cent doing most of the work (Nielsen). People move along a path from reader to contributor to collaborator to leader rather than arriving as one or the other (Preece and Shneiderman). And they learn how to belong by hovering at the edge and copying what they see before they join in, which is why what a newcomer can see in their first minute decides almost everything that follows (Lave and Wenger's legitimate peripheral participation).

So the unit of analysis is a person climbing, not a department maturing. That is also why it sells: a buyer who does not know what community means has no opinion about their own maturity, but they immediately recognise a member who walked in and left.

the test

The Drop-Off Test

The diagnostic is the engine of the whole business. It does three jobs at once: it teaches the market what community means, it identifies the buyer's rung, and it establishes the baseline that makes impact pricing possible at all. Without it you are back to selling hours.

Two questions per rung, answered by the owner, plus the matching number pulled from their platform where you can get it. Output is one sentence: you are stuck on rung two, in The Void, and here is the one thing to do about it. Ten questions, two minutes, free.

What makes it defensible rather than a quiz

Every test you run adds a community to your own distribution. After ten, the report stops saying this looks low to me and starts saying your first-post response rate is in the bottom quartile of the communities I have measured. Nobody who has not run the tests can say that sentence, and it gets stronger every time you use it. The framework is your marketing; the accumulated benchmark is the part that is hard to copy.

the offer

Selling the rung, not the month

Here is the mechanism that turns this from a retainer with better branding into impact pricing: the unit of sale is one rung.

The contract names the rung, the number that defines it, the baseline you measured, the target, the window, and what happens if it does not move. A retainer sells presence. This sells a delta, and the delta is checkable by both parties using the same test that produced the baseline.

The guarantee is what makes it impact pricing

Pick one and write it down: a stated share refunded if the number does not move, or thirty further days at no charge. It is the difference between claiming to sell outcomes and selling them. It is also only survivable because the ladder makes the work predictable — you are not promising a thriving community, you are promising one number on one rung inside ninety days.

delivery

What actually gets handed over

A productised service is one where the deliverable is the same every time. Five kits, one per rung, each a small set of artefacts rather than a set of meetings.

  1. 01

    Door Kit

    The one-question doorway, the first-message script, the channel cull list, and a rewritten first-minute view of the space.

  2. 02

    Greeter Kit

    The rota template, the response-time promise, four reply patterns that are not “welcome!”, and a weekly unanswered-posts check.

  3. 03

    Rhythm Kit

    One ritual designed end to end: format, cue, announcement, recap, and the member who eventually owns it. Plus the day-three and day-ten nudge scripts.

  4. 04

    Introduction Kit

    The weekly pairing process, the thirty-minute staff delay rule, and the map of who currently talks to whom, with the isolated regulars listed by name.

  5. 05

    Handover Kit

    Role definitions with one responsibility each, the norms document owned by moderators, the graduated-consequences ladder, and the announced absence plan.

Each kit is a file, not a call. That is what makes the ninetieth delivery cost less than the first, and it is what a certified implementer is eventually handed.

sequencing

The order to build this in

The usual failure is building the course first. Do it backwards.

  1. 1

    Months 1–3 · Use your two clients as the laboratory

    You are already doing rungs one to four for two completely different audiences. Write down what you do, with the rung names attached, as you do it. Measure before and after. That is your v1 and your first two case studies, and it costs you no extra time because you are being paid for the work anyway.

  2. 2

    Month 2 · Publish the definition

    One page: what community actually means, in five rungs. Free, public, no gate. This is the answer to the question your whole market is asking, and being the person who has an answer is the entire positioning.

  3. 3

    Month 3 · Ship the test and run it for free, twenty times

    Cold outreach stops being a pitch and becomes a finding: three per cent of your eight thousand members have ever posted. Twenty free tests gets you the benchmark, the list, and the content.

  4. 4

    Months 4–6 · Sell three One Rung engagements at a discount

    Explicitly priced low in exchange for the case study and the right to publish the numbers. Three completed rungs with before-and-after figures is what makes the full price defensible.

  5. 5

    Months 7–12 · Raise the price, add The Watch, write the kits properly

    By now the delivery is repeatable enough that the margin comes from the kits rather than from your presence.

  6. 6

    Month 12+ · Certify, once and not before

    Ten completed engagements with movement you can show. Not earlier. A certification built on an unproven method is the fastest way to destroy the framework's credibility permanently.

honesty

Four things that will go wrong

A framework is not a moat

Anyone can copy five rungs once they know which five. What cannot be copied is the measured benchmark, the case studies, and the fact of having done it. All three come from running engagements, so the framework is the marketing and the delivery is the defence.

Impact pricing dies without measurement

If you cannot produce a defensible baseline, the guarantee becomes an argument and you will lose it. The diagnostic is load-bearing, not a marketing toy, and it needs to work on data the client can actually give you.

Rung five is the one clients will not buy and most need

Handing the community over reduces the founder's centrality, and many will resist it while saying they want it. Sell it as insurance and as an asset valuation rather than as delegation.

Two employer-shaped clients is why there is no time to build this

Early mornings, the middle of the day and late evenings is not a foundation, it is the reason nothing gets built. One of the two becomes the laboratory. The other gets deliberately shrunk on a date you choose in advance, rather than on the day it becomes unbearable.

where this comes from

The mechanisms are established work: self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan), sense of community (McMillan and Chavis, 1986), the evidence review in Kraut and Resnick's Building Successful Online Communities, reader-to-leader (Preece and Shneiderman, 2009), legitimate peripheral participation (Lave and Wenger, 1991), implementation intentions (Gollwitzer), the Fogg behaviour model, weak ties (Granovetter, 1973), Dunbar's layers, and Ostrom's design principles for durable commons. What is new here is only the arrangement, the naming, and the pricing mechanism.
